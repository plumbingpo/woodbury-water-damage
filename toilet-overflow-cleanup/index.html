Dealing With Toilet Overflow Cleanup in Woodbury

A toilet overflow looks like a mop job, but the water does not stay on the tile. It runs under the baseboard, into the wall and through the gap around the drain pipe. Most important is what the water was carrying. Clean water is what comes out of the supply line or the tank. Bowl water is not clean, so we handle it as contaminated from the start.

In Woodbury, we begin each overflow job by determining the category of water, which decides how the rest is done. With clean water, the job is extraction followed by drying. For gray or black water we wear protective gear, remove soaked porous items such as carpet pad, and disinfect each surface the water reached. After that, moisture meters go on the wall bases, the subfloor and the room below to make sure no wet spot remains.

First Five Minutes

Before We Arrive

  1. Shut Off the SupplyBehind the toilet is a small valve, and turning it fully clockwise shuts the water off. A stuck valve can be worked around by taking off the tank lid and lifting the float, which stops the fill.
  2. Do Not Flush AgainHold off on flushing, and keep the washer, showers and sinks off too. If the drain line is blocked, any water you send down can come back up.
  3. Stay Out of Contaminated WaterIf the water came from the bowl or contains waste, keep children and pets out, and wear gloves and waterproof shoes if you must walk through it.
  4. Look at the Ceiling UnderneathWith a second floor bathroom, inspect the ceiling of the room beneath. If it is dripping or sagging, switch off power to that room and move belongings out of the way.
  5. Contain Clean WaterLay down old towels so clean water does not travel into hallways or onto carpet. Anything contaminated is a job for people with proper gear and disinfecting products.
Common Causes

Why This Happens

  • A stuck flapper or fill valve that keeps the tank running without stopping.
  • A sewer line narrowed by tree roots or a collapse, so every drain in the home runs slow.
  • A cracked tank or failed supply line that floods the bathroom with clean water.
  • Blocked traps and drain lines, which leave the bowl nowhere to empty but onto the floor.
  • A main sewer line blockage that backs waste up through the lowest toilet.
If It Is Left

Where the Damage Spreads

Bacteria in Building Materials

Water from the drain or bowl deposits bacteria in drywall, baseboards, carpet and pad. Porous materials cannot be reliably disinfected and usually have to be removed.

Ceiling Below

In a second story bathroom, water travels down along the drain pipe and collects on top of the ceiling drywall below, which stains, sags and in some cases collapses.

Vanity and Drywall Wicking

The bottom of the drywall and the toe kick of the vanity take in water and pull it upward. The wall can be wet several inches above the floor while the paint still looks fine.

Swollen Baseboards and Trim

Sitting at floor level, door casings and baseboards wick water up from their lower edge. Since trim is usually fiberboard, it puffs up, cracks apart and stays deformed.

Coverage

Insurance and Toilet Overflow Cleanup

When an overflow is accidental and sudden, like a clog nobody expected or a broken fill valve, homeowners policies often cover it. Sewer or drain backup is often excluded without a backup endorsement on the policy, and an overflow from a blocked main line can fall under that exclusion. If the toilet leaked at its base for months, that damage is usually not covered. Read your policy or call your agent to find out which situation applies to you.

Pricing

What Affects the Cost

  • Whether the toilet or vanity has to be pulled to dry and clean beneath it.
  • How far the water traveled, such as into a hallway, bedroom carpet or closet.
  • Whether the water was contaminated, because those overflows take more protection, disinfection and labor.
  • Whether the toilet is upstairs and the ceiling or walls below got wet.
